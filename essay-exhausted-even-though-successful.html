Carrying

Why do I feel exhausted even though I’m successful?

You’re not alone, and you’re not broken. You’ve earned your seat, so stop doing everyone else’s job from it.
Amanda Friedt

Bestie, you’re not alone. You’re successful, you’re exhausted, and some days the whole thing is a hot mess. Same. We’re in it together, so let’s talk about what’s actually going on.

There’s a kind of work that leaves very little evidence behind. The meeting happened. The deadline held. Everybody got what they needed. You scheduled it, chased the updates, and remembered the thing everyone agreed to three Tuesdays ago.

On paper, it worked. You’re still thinking about it at dinner.

I know that dinner. In 2019 I was in so many meetings I never ate, barely used the restroom, worked sixty-plus hours a week on average. One month I clocked three hundred hours. Three hundred.

That’s not a confession. It’s a receipt. You’ve probably got one just like it, and it doesn’t mean you’re doing it wrong. It means you’re the one everybody counts on, and you’ve earned your seat.

I keep coming back to the difference between caring and carrying. I can care about a thing enough to show up for it and still ask who’s responsible for what happens after I leave. That sounds obvious until you’re about to type “I’ll just do it.”

Carrying doesn’t show from the outside. There is a particular loneliness to being publicly fine. In 2023 I wrote it in my journal: “The hardest days were the ones nobody could see.”

The fix started when I said it out loud. On one call I finally admitted it: “I feel like everything I’m doing right now is failing and this year has kicked my ass.” And then I heard the sigh of relief at the end of the phone. Me too.

That sigh is the whole point of this page. A lot of the women you admire are holding the same weight and smiling through it. Once somebody says it out loud, you stop pretending and start deciding.

And no, the answer isn’t to get smaller. Executive presence doesn’t mean shrinking so nobody gets uncomfortable. It means knowing what’s yours and saying it clearly. It also means not taking the emotional bait when someone tries to hand you their mess on a three-hour call.

Now the practical part. The next time your fingers start typing “I’ll just do it,” try something smaller. Name the task. Then name all the work around it. Who remembers? Who follows up? Who notices if it goes sideways? Now ask who actually agreed to own it.

For years I asked the operator’s question. How do I get all of this done? It is a faithful question. It is also a treadmill, because how do I get it done accepts the workload as given and volunteers your body as the solution. Ask it long enough and you will optimize yourself into the most efficient exhausted woman in the county, which I was, with documentation.

The question that changed everything was the one I had been asking professionally for years and had never once aimed at my own life. How should this be designed?

So design it. You already do this for your team and your family all week. Trust that you know how, and point it at your own calendar.

I made Carrying vs. Caring because I wanted somewhere to practice that question before I needed it in a real conversation. It’s fourteen days of small exercises. We’re starting with a live session called Whose Is This? Bring one thing you keep catching and we’ll work through it together.

I want you to leave with words you can use. I also want you to put one thing on your calendar that’s yours because you wanted it there.

Rest is neutral, not risky.

Questions women ask me

If this sounds like you.

Is this high-functioning burnout?

Maybe, but for a lot of high performers it's something else. You've been running everyone else's operation on top of your own, and the fix is handing back what was never yours. If the exhaustion doesn't lift with rest, or it's showing up in your sleep, your mood or your health, talk to a doctor or a therapist. This page is about the load, not a diagnosis.

Why am I so tired when nothing is technically wrong?

Because the visible task is rarely the whole job. Around every meeting and deadline there is remembering, reminding, following up and noticing when something is about to slip. That work leaves almost no evidence, so it is easy to do all day and still feel like you did nothing.

How do I get my boss to respect my boundaries?

Skip the apology and name the ownership. Bosses respond to clarity about the work. Say what you will deliver and who takes it from there: "I can send the notes today. Can we agree who's tracking the actions after that?" If everything is a priority, ask your boss to rank them, then hold to the ranking.

What is the difference between caring and carrying?

Caring is showing up for something because it matters to you. Carrying is holding the responsibility for what happens after you leave, often for work nobody actually asked you to own. You can care deeply about a project, a team or a family and still set down the parts that belong to someone else.
