Becoming

The Receiving Margin

For two months I wrote "I am open to receive" in my own journal. It took 328 photographs to notice where I wrote it.
Amanda Farley

It was almost midnight on the first of July, and I was standing over my kitchen table photographing journal pages, one at a time, like evidence.

Three hundred and twenty-eight photos before I was done. Two months of pages — May and June — moon spreads and gates and tapping scripts, four colors of ink, stickers my youngest would recognize, roses doodled into every corner because my hand draws them now without asking me first. I wasn't writing that night. I was doing something I'd never actually done before, which was reading myself. Not a page here and there. All of it, in order, like a stranger would.

And a stranger would notice something in about four minutes that took me two months and a camera roll to see.

The center of my pages is loud. The center is where the crowns live. I am perfect in my enoughness. I am a teacher and a leader. I choose to claim that I AM the one. There is a page from May where I wrote — in pen, where God and my grandchildren can someday find it — I am the one, The GOAT. I'm not going to pretend I'm embarrassed. Some seasons you write like that because you're building a woman out of sentences and the load-bearing ones have to be big.

But the margins.

The margins are where the other handwriting lives. Smaller. Sideways. Squeezed between a sticker and the edge of the paper like it was hoping not to be called on. I am open to receive. Ask for help. A little list of names with arrows pointing toward me — the people I could lean on, drawn like a diagram of a thing I understood in theory. Those lines are everywhere in those two months. And not once — I went back and checked, that's what the 328 photographs were for — not once do they appear in the center of a page.

The center is where I put what I believe. The margin is where I put what I'm still practicing.

I used to think the journal was one conversation. It isn't. The page has a geography, and the geography doesn't lie. The center is where I put what I believe. The margin is where I put what I'm not ready to let be true at full size.

There's a page from early May that I can barely look at now, and it's not even one of the dramatic ones. It's a printed page of journal questions, the kind that comes in the workbook, and the first question asks: Do I ask for help when I need it? Why or why not?

My answer, in blue ink, four words: I need to ask more consistently.

That's it. That's the whole answer. A woman who can fill a margin about faeries and yachts and the theory of momentum gave the shortest answer of the month to that one. And then — this is the part that got me — two questions down, the page asks: Do I believe that I deserve to be supported?

And I wrote: yes, I am doing a good job even when it feels challenging and overwhelming.

Read that again, because I had to. The question was about deserving support. I answered with a performance review. Nobody asked me for a status report. I filed one anyway — proof of work, attached to a question about being loved. Somewhere along the way, some part of me decided that support was a wage. That it had to be earned in the quarter it was received. That the answer to do you deserve to be held was let me show you my numbers.

I know where that part was built. If you've read anything else I've written, you know I spent years being the glue, the reliable one, the woman whose competence became the family's infrastructure and then the company's and then everyone's. I have written a great deal about learning to put down what was never mine to carry. I thought that was the whole lesson. I thought when my hands finally opened, the work was done.

It turns out an open hand has two jobs, and I had only trained it for one.

Letting go, I've practiced. Receiving — letting something land, letting it stay, letting it be given without auditing whether I'd earned it by Friday — that muscle never got built, because carrying never needed it. Carrying is a one-way valve. Things flow in; nothing is allowed to flow back, because the moment you receive, you are visible. You are witnessable. Someone has seen that you needed, and now you're standing there with your hands out and no invoice to hide behind.

I answered a question about deserving support with a performance review. Nobody asked me for a status report. I filed one anyway.

In June I wrote a line in the middle of a page about sharing and resources, and I don't think I understood it when I wrote it: Receiving requires release. I underlined it and kept moving, the way you do when something true gets ahead of you. But that's the entire mechanism, right there. Receiving isn't a worthiness problem. I believe I'm worthy — it's in the center of every page, wearing a crown. Receiving is a release problem. To let something be given to you, there is a moment — brief, wild, completely unsupervised — where the thing is out of your hands and you cannot manage its arrival. You can't earn it faster. You can't quality-check it midair. You just have to stand there, open, while it comes. For a woman who kept a whole life aloft by never letting a single plate wobble, that moment doesn't feel like grace. It feels like falling.

So we file it in the margin. Women like me — and I've met enough of us now to know the handwriting on sight — we keep our receiving in the smallest font we own. We'll write ask for help sideways, in the gutter, next to the sticker. We'll make the list of people who love us and draw the arrows and never place the call. Not because we don't believe we deserve it. Because the giver's seat is the safest seat in the room, and we got there early, and we've held it so long the whole room assumes it's assigned.

I started calling it the receiving margin. The receiving margin is the gap between what you'll do for everyone and what you'll let anyone do for you — and you can read it, literally, in where things land on your own pages. What you believe goes in the center. What you're still practicing hides at the edge.

I keep thinking about what else is living out there in my margins. That's the question the photographs left me with, and I'll leave it with you the way it was left with me, which is to say: uninvited, at midnight, in your own handwriting. Somewhere there is a sentence you have been writing small for years because you are not ready for it to be true at full size.

Mine had been in the margin since May.

So a few nights ago, I opened the journal to a fresh page for July. No gate, no moon, no stickers yet. And in the middle of the page, in the handwriting I use for crowns, I wrote it at full size: I am open to receive.

It looked enormous. It looked like furniture in the wrong room. I sat there with the pen and the urge to add something after it — a qualifier, a to-do list, a little proof I'd earned the line. I didn't. I closed the book.

The roses can have the margins.
