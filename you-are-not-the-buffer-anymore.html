You Are Not the Buffer Anymore

How to stop absorbing consequences that were never yours.

Amanda Friedt · The Unapologetic Leader

The idea, in three sentences

A buffer is what happens when your competence gets positioned between other people and the consequences of their own choices. Buffers don’t just absorb impact. They delete the feedback a choice would otherwise produce, which is why nothing around you ever changes. You can care deeply without carrying it all.

The phone was already in my hand.

Not to fix anything yet. Just to be ready. That was always step one, being ready before I was even asked.

The message said the same few words it always said right before something broke.

Old me had a whole sequence for this. Call ahead. Buy the extra day. Write the explanation that made carelessness sound like a scheduling conflict. Absorb the awkward conversation so someone else didn’t have to sit in it.

I started typing. Then I stopped. Not because I stopped caring. Because I finally saw what I was doing. I was building a soft landing for a fall that belonged to someone else.

I deleted the message and put the phone face down on the counter.

I used to think peace meant preventing the explosion.

So I became very good at spotting the spark before anyone else did. I could hear the shift in tone. See the missed detail. Feel the mood change before it became a problem. Predict the fallout before anyone admitted fallout was coming.

And because I could see it, I thought it was my job to manage it. Smooth it over. Explain it better. Make the plan. Absorb the reaction. Keep the room from feeling what was actually happening.

For a long time, I called that maturity.

It’s worth saying where the skill comes from, because it isn’t random. When you’re raised in survival mode, staying a step ahead is how you stayed safe. You learned to read a room before you learned it was optional. Carry that into leadership, into marriage, into life, and you arrive pretrained: get there first, smooth it early, avoid the headache. From the outside it looks like conscientiousness. Underneath, it’s old code that never got the message the danger passed. And nobody counts the mental load it takes to stay permanently ahead of everyone else’s next mistake, doing things for people so they never have to worry, holding a running forecast for every person in the room. That’s not a to-do list. That’s a second, invisible job.

Here’s the word I didn’t have for myself back then: buffer.

A buffer is what happens when your competence gets positioned between other people and the consequences of their own choices. Buffers don’t just absorb impact. They delete information.

That second sentence took me years to understand, so let me slow it down.

A consequence is not a punishment. It’s a signal. It’s the only honest feedback a choice ever produces, the moment reality reports back. When you absorb that consequence, the signal never reaches the person who generated it. You didn’t just take the hit. You erased the data.

Which explains the thing that used to bewilder me: why nothing ever changed. I softened the message, covered the gap, paid the cost, explained the behavior. And the behavior kept arriving on schedule. I assumed I wasn’t helping enough. The truth was simpler and worse. From where they stood, nothing ever went wrong. I was personally financing their version of reality, and their version of reality had no problems in it.

Motherhood taught me this long before I let it apply to adults. You tell a kid not to jump on the couch. You pull them back from the street, and you should. That part is the job. But every mother eventually learns the difference between the falls that need her hands and the falls that are the teacher. The couch lesson never comes from the warning. It comes from the couch. You can repeat yourself for a year, or the floor can explain it once.

Adults are no different. We just call the couch something else. When I absorbed a consequence, I wasn’t just exhausting myself. I was robbing someone of a lesson they needed. Not maliciously. Lovingly. But robbed is robbed.

I did this in my marriage too. For years I tried to protect him from the weight of his own choices. Softened it. Managed it. Took the hit so he wouldn’t have to. It took me far too long to learn the sentence I would now hand any woman doing the same thing: you cannot love someone into accountability. You can only stop standing in for it.

I want to be careful here, because I know how this reads if you’re still in the middle of it. It reads cold. It felt cold to me the first time I let it happen. But there is a difference between supporting someone through a consequence and standing between them and it. Support says: I’m with you while this lands. Buffering says: this will never land while I’m here. Only one of those treats the other person as someone capable of learning. Letting a consequence land is not the withdrawal of love. Sometimes it’s the most respectful thing you can offer another adult.

It took me even longer to see the buffering as a failure of service, because service was the language I had used to justify it. I thought absorbing things was what serving looked like. It isn’t. Real service builds someone’s strength to carry what is theirs. There’s an old distinction between helping to carry the burden that would crush a person and carrying the load that was theirs to grow under. I had collapsed the two for years. When I stood between a person and their consequence, I wasn’t serving anyone. I was interrupting a conversation that was never mine, the one between that person and the life that is trying to teach them. Some lessons have an author, and it isn’t me. The most faithful thing I know how to do now is step out of the way of what someone’s life is trying to tell them, and stay close enough to love them while they hear it.

And I believe in grace. Everyone gets a bad week, a dropped ball, a season they can’t carry themselves. Grace covers falls. But I gave grace, and then grace, and then grace again, and they showed me, and showed me, and showed me. At some point the pattern is the answer. Grace was never meant to fund the same choice on repeat. When someone shows you the same thing enough times, the most gracious thing you have left is to move out of the way and let them fall.

So what kept me in position all those years? Not martyrdom. A forecast. Somewhere along the way I had built a catastrophe model, a detailed internal simulation of what would happen if I didn’t move first. It was vivid. It was also old, and I had never once tested it against actual weather.

Then the consequence landed, and it was smaller than I had spent years imagining. Nobody died. Nobody even really got mad. A thing that was supposed to happen on time happened late, and the person it happened to felt it.

That was it. That was the whole disaster I had reorganized my life to prevent.

Nobody taught me this part. Sometimes maturity is not stepping in faster. Sometimes maturity is letting the truth arrive without volunteering your body as the landing pad.

You can love people and still stop protecting them from reality.
You can be kind and still stop translating disrespect into confusion.
You can want peace and still refuse to be the buffer between someone’s choices and their consequences.

This is why I teach it now. Not only so you can find your own peace and get out of survival, though that matters. Because somewhere, someone’s lesson is being held hostage by your competence. Putting down their weight is not abandoning them. It’s giving the lesson back to the conversation it always belonged to.

Try this before the week is out. Find one consequence you’re currently scheduled to absorb. You’ll know it because you’re already braced for it. Don’t announce anything. Don’t warn anyone. Just don’t move first. Then write down two things: what your forecast said would happen, and what actually did. Keep both.

And one question worth sitting with: whose consequence am I carrying right now, and what signal is it deleting?

Peace did not come from keeping everything from breaking.

Peace came when I stopped breaking myself first.
